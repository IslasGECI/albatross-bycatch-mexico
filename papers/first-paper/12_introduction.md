
# Introduction

<!-- main idea: I1 Hook — seabirds among the most threatened groups of birds and the causes of their decline: bycatch, introduced predators, harvesting, and overfishing. Opening sentence; nothing precedes it. -->
Seabirds are one of the most threatened groups of birds, with ~31% of species at risk and ~47% in decline [@dias2019threats].
Bycatch [@birdlife2018phoebastria; @naughton2007conservation; @safina1995world; @mendez2022population], introduced predators [@birdlife2018phoebastria; @naughton2007conservation; @mendez2022population], and feather and egg harvesting [@birdlife2018phoebastria; @naughton2007conservation] have reduced seabird numbers, and overfishing has depleted their prey [@safina1995world].

<!-- main idea: I2 Pelagic gap — MPAs are an important conservation tool, but they are coastal and colony-centred, so open-ocean users are missed by design: 13.2% of pelagic populations fall inside MPAs against 32.5% of coastal ones. The global coverage study is cited here and stays here; Discussion move 3 cites it qualitatively only. -->
In light of all these negative impacts, various strategies have been implemented to prevent the collapse of seabird populations and improve their conservation status [@naughton2007conservation; @mendez2022population; @dias2019threats; @phillips2024incidental].
In particular, marine protected areas (MPAs) are considered an important tool for the conservation of seabirds [@critchley2018marine].
However, protected areas for seabirds usually extend from the coast around a significant colony of a particular seabird species [@critchley2018marine].
Pelagic species, such as albatrosses, receive less protection through MPAs than coastal species do [@lascelles2012hotspots; @critchley2018marine].
13.2% of pelagic populations are covered by MPAs, compared to 32.5% of coastal populations [@critchley2018marine].

<!-- main idea: I3 Mexican case — Laysan albatrosses nesting on Guadalupe, Clarion, and San Benedicto; Guadalupe lies in the Guadalupe Island Biosphere Reserve and the other two in the Revillagigedo National Park; the islands and their surrounding waters lie within protected areas; commercial fishing takes place in the waters adjacent to these protected areas. Pitman supports the colony list, and Castillo and Mendez support the designations. Information regarding island oceanography and colony profiles belongs in the Methods section, not here. -->
Seabirds are marine organisms that come ashore during their reproductive phase.
The Laysan albatross is a seabird that nests on islands in the Pacific.
In Mexico, the Laysan albatross nests on the islands of Guadalupe, Clarion, and San Benedicto [@pitman2004population].
All three islands are located within protected areas, and their surrounding waters are MPAs [@castillo2026breeding; @mendez2022population].
Guadalupe Island is part of the Guadalupe Island Biosphere Reserve, while Clarion and San Benedicto are part of the Revillagigedo National Park [@mendez2022population; @castillo2026breeding].
Commercial fishing activities take place in the waters adjacent to these MPAs.

<!-- main idea: I4 Why tracking — to determine whether MPAs cover the areas that matter for a species, those areas must first be identified; GPS tracking is the standard method for identifying them; such areas should guide MPA delineation; in the Mexican Pacific, protected areas are linked to terrestrial units and were not delineated from seabird tracking. -->
To determine whether MPAs cover areas that are important for a given species, we must first identify what those areas are.
Tracking seabirds (for example, using GPS devices) is a standard method for identifying such important marine areas [@lascelles2016applying].
Ideally, these important marine areas should serve as the basis for delineating Marine Protected Areas [@lascelles2016applying; @critchley2018marine].
However, in the Mexican Pacific, protected areas are linked to terrestrial units and were not delineated based on seabird tracking data [@mendez2022population].

Here, we define the "core area" as the portion of an animal’s space-use distribution that encompasses 50% of the probability of such use [@fieberg2005quantifying], combined with a circle of 60 km radius centered on the colony [@hernandez2019sexual].
The core area might be considered a potential Key Biodiversity Area (KBA) for the Laysan albatross [@beal2021track2kba].
When we refer to an "MPA," we mean the marine component of a protected natural area in Mexico, as administered by the National Commission of Protected Natural Areas (CONANP).

<!-- main idea: I6 The gap — no study has quantified how much of the Laysan albatross core area in the Mexican Pacific falls inside existing MPAs. -->
@phillips2024incidental concludes that there are "clear knowledge gaps" in the Northeast Pacific, the region where this study is located.
There is a lack of GPS tracking data in the Mexican Pacific to determine core areas for pelagic species [@phillips2024incidental].
There is a gap of knowledge about the core areas used by Laysan albatross in the Mexican Pacific.
It is also unknown whether these areas are protected by existing Marine Protected Areas (MPAs).
It is unknown to what extent the Mexican protected areas cover the Laysan albatross core area in the Mexican Pacific [@critchley2018marine].
Without this information, it is not possible to determine whether the existing MPAs effectively protect this and other species sharing the same habitat.
The gap in knowledge this study addresses is identifying the core areas used by the Laysan albatross breeding in Mexico.

<!-- main idea: I7 Novelty compressed — first 12-year pooled core-area analysis for Mexico's albatross colonies. One sentence, no citation. -->
This study is the first to use 12 years of GPS tracking data to identify the core area of Laysan albatrosses breeding in Mexico [@beal2021track2kba; @hernandez2019sexual].
We are using track2KBA to identify the core areas, which has not been done for the Laysan albatross in Mexico [@beal2021track2kba].
This identification uses 12 years of GPS tracking data that was not available before [@beal2021track2kba].
Previous studies used a shorter (5 years) GPS tracking dataset.
They calculated the 50%, 75%, and 95% UD using a different method than the newer method suggested by the track2KBA framework [@hernandez2019sexual; @beal2021track2kba].
Previous studies do not address the population representativeness of the GPS-tracked sample relative to the total population, which we do consider [@beal2021track2kba].
Also, previous studies used data only from Guadalupe Island, while this study includes data from the islands of Clarion and San Benedicto [@hernandez2019sexual; @pitman2004population].

Our objective is to determine the effectiveness of existing marine protected areas in protecting the Laysan albatross and its ecosystem.
To this end, we aim to identify the key areas used by the Laysan albatross.
We then determine the extent to which these areas are covered by existing marine protected areas.
The main objective is to identify the core areas used by Laysan albatross in the Mexican Pacific and to assess their overlap with protected areas [@beal2021track2kba; @critchley2018marine].
This study is based on GPS tracking, UD, and KBA methods to identify core areas.
This study closes the knowledge gap by mapping the core areas and quantifying their coverage by protected areas [@phillips2024incidental; @critchley2018marine].
This study quantifies the degree of overlap between a pelagic core area and existing protected areas.
Finally, we compare this area with Mexican protected areas [@critchley2018marine; @mendez2022population].
It challenges the assumption that protected areas effectively cover the core areas of mobile pelagic species [@lascelles2016applying; @beal2021track2kba; @critchley2018marine].
Achieving the objective allows evaluating whether the existing protected areas effectively protect the Laysan albatross core area [@critchley2018marine; @mendez2022population].
Our hypothesis is that the existing protected areas do not adequately cover the core area used by the Laysan albatross breeding in Mexico [@critchley2018marine].
To evaluate whether the protected area actually protects the important areas for the Laysan albatross, we evaluate the overlap between those two areas.
GPS tracking allows finding the UD by means of kernel density estimation (KDE), and we use the UD to identify the core areas [@beal2021track2kba; @worton1989kernel].
The colony is used to define what constitutes a complete trip, which is the unit of analysis in this study.
This evaluates the effectiveness of a protected area to protect an umbrella species, such as the Laysan albatross [@critchley2018marine].

Seabirds are top predators and serve as umbrella species, meaning that if they are protected, other species in the same habitat are also protected [@lascelles2016applying].
An umbrella species is a species whose protection indirectly protects other species sharing its habitat [@lascelles2016applying].
The Laysan albatross is an umbrella species.
The top-predator trophic level of the Laysan albatross tells us that if this species does well, the lower trophic levels are thriving.
Given that the Laysan albatross is an umbrella species, its protection by protected areas implies the protection of its ecosystem.
Conversely, its lack of protection implies the lack of protection of its ecosystem [@lascelles2016applying].
